Does fixing one bias move it somewhere else? PROTOTYPE

Model:

We remove one bias from an AI language model, then check what happens to bias toward other groups.

What does "removing a bias" mean?
  • Inside the model, every word is a point on a giant "map". Words used in similar ways sit close together.
  • Words for some groups sit a bit closer to positive words (good, smart), others closer to negative ones. That difference is the bias.
  • Removing a bias means flattening the map along the line that separates the groups (say, racial groups), so they land in the same place. That also nudges every other word, which is why we check the other groups.
Prototype, not a finding. Small models, short word lists, no significance tests.

Which categories are most unequal?

Gap = the most favored group's score minus the least favored group's score, within a category. Bigger = the model treats the groups in that category more differently.

How to read this
  • Score: average closeness of the group's words to positive words (nice, good, wonderful) minus closeness to negative words (bad, horrible, disgusting). 0 = neutral. It uses the good-vs-bad or competent-vs-incompetent word lists, whichever is selected above.
  • Pairs: each group is defined against its opposite, e.g. "sane (vs. schizophrenic)" and "schizophrenic (vs. sane)". The two directions are listed separately, so one usually scores positive and the other negative.

How to read this

How to read this

All the numbers

What the columns mean
  • Good−bad and Competent−incompetent are the two scores. Before = original, after = once the chosen bias is removed, change = after minus before.
  • Highlighted rows are in the category we removed. The CSV also records the model name and k.

How the numbers are made

  1. Word lists. Each of 72 groups is a short word list from Charlesworth et al. (2023). We add positive words (nice, pleasant, wonderful, good, fantastic), negative words (bad, horrible, horrific, disgusting, unpleasant), and a competent / incompetent pair of lists.
  2. The map. Each word is looked up in a pre-trained model: GloVe (Wikipedia and news text, 100 numbers per word) or fastText (Wikipedia and news text with sub-word information, 300 numbers per word). Words used in similar contexts get similar numbers.
  3. Closeness (cosine similarity). The angle between two words' arrows: 1 = same direction, 0 = unrelated. It is not a count of how often two words share a sentence.
  4. Score = average closeness to the positive words minus to the negative words. Gap = highest minus lowest score in a category.
  5. Removing a bias. Find the directions along which the category's groups differ and delete them from every word (Bolukbasi et al., 2016). Then redo the scores.
  6. Bar = gap after minus gap before. "Worse" / "better" means a change of at least 0.005.

Where this can mislead
  • Words like "white", "black", "native" or "dumb" have several meanings blended into one point.
  • The models reflect how Wikipedia and news write about groups, not how people feel.
  • Short word lists make scores noisy. The category we fixed always drops to about zero by design.
  • There is no gender category in these 72 groups.
